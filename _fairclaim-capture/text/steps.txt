Five steps, from asking to getting paid.

Each step costs time, and some cost money. The last, collecting, is the one people most often underestimate.

BEFORE YOU DECIDE

Court is one option among several.

Often quicker and cheaper, and some end the dispute for good.

A
Talk or negotiate

A phone call or a meeting can settle things faster than a court date. Some people offer to accept part of the amount, or to be paid in installments. Put any agreement in writing.

B
Send a demand letter

A clear written request with a deadline. Many disputes end here, and if they don't, the letter is a record that you asked.

C
Mediation

A neutral person helps both sides reach their own agreement. Many courts and community programs offer it free or at low cost, before or instead of a hearing.

D
A complaint to a regulator

If their work needs a state license, the licensing agency may take complaints, and so does your state attorney general's consumer protection office. They don't always get your money back, but some businesses respond.

E
Insurance or a card dispute

For damage to a car or home, your own insurance or the other side's may cover it. If you paid by credit card, ask the card company whether you can dispute the charge.

F
Let it go

Sometimes the time, cost and stress of chasing the money are more than the money. Writing it off is a reasonable choice, too.

COSTS THAT DON'T SHOW UP ON A FEE SCHEDULE

01
Time

Preparing papers and evidence, arranging service, a half or full day at court, and sometimes months of collection work afterward.

02
Stress

Facing the other side in person, waiting for a decision, and the chance of losing.

03
Relationships

Suing a neighbor, landlord, relative or former client usually ends the relationship.

04
Money you may not get back

Filing and service fees, time off work, and collection fees if they don't pay.

The other side can make a claim too.

The person you sue may be able to file their own counterclaim in the same case. If the judge agrees with them, you could end up owing money.

An example from California
Winning and getting paid are different things.

A judgment says you're owed money. It doesn't make the other side pay. Studies of small claims courts found many winners collected little or nothing.
